Clinical Approach

  • Do I need imaging — an X-ray or MRI — before I start?

    No. The majority of conditions can be assessed and treated from clinical examination alone. If you have existing imaging, bring it — it provides useful context. But imaging is not a prerequisite to start, and waiting for a scan before beginning treatment is rarely necessary or beneficial. Clinical examination tells us what the tissue is doing under load; an image tells us what it looks like at rest. Those are different questions.

  • I've tried physiotherapy before and it didn't help. Why would this be different?

    The most common reason physiotherapy doesn't produce lasting results is that treatment addressed the symptom without rebuilding the capacity. Pain was reduced — through manual therapy, passive modalities, or generic exercise — but the load tolerance was never restored. When the load returned, the pain returned. The approach here is different: assessment identifies the capacity deficit, treatment builds it systematically, and discharge happens when the tissue can handle the demands being placed on it — not when pain is temporarily absent.

  • How will I know when I'm ready to return to sport or full training?

    Readiness is determined by clinical criteria — not a calendar, not how the area feels, and not how many sessions have passed. Criteria include strength benchmarks, load tolerance under sport-specific demands, limb symmetry testing where relevant, and movement quality maintained under fatigue. You will know you are ready because you will have demonstrated it — not assumed it. This is the core difference between symptom-based and criteria-based rehabilitation.

Recovery

  • How many sessions will I need?

    This cannot be answered accurately before assessment. The number of sessions depends on the condition, chronicity, your load history, and your goals. After session one, you will have an honest estimate — not a generic number designed to fill a schedule.

  • How long will rehabilitation take?

    Timelines vary significantly by case. An acute sports injury with clear mechanics may progress faster than a chronic presentation with layered tissue and movement dysfunction. After assessment, you will receive an honest and specific timeline for your case.

  • Can I keep training during rehabilitation?

    In most cases, yes — often with modifications. Complete rest is rarely the best approach. Training continues with specific guidance on what to modify, what to avoid temporarily, and what to add for accelerated tissue recovery.

  • What if I am not sure what is wrong?

    That is a normal starting point. The clinical assessment is designed to identify exactly what is happening and why. You do not need to arrive with a diagnosis — that is part of what session one is for.
